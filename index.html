    The story · from the source document

    How the 6F drawings became a graph

    This page retells From As-Built Drawings to a Knowledge Graph section by section. Section numbers match the PDF. Numbers quoted here come from the PDF; nothing has been added to them.

    Sections
    §1

    A chiller trips. Which rooms get hot?

    On a summer afternoon, one of the chillers on the roof of the research building trips. The first question the facility staff must answer is simple: which rooms will get hot, and whom should we notify?

    Answering with drawings means flipping through four sheets

    1. Equipment schedule: this chiller is CH-1, and here is its capacity.
    2. System flow diagram: which floors does CH-1’s chilled water reach through the main riser? Floors 3F to 9F.
    3. HVAC plan for each floor: where each fan coil unit is installed.
    4. Architectural plan for each floor: which room that location is, and what it is called.

    The sixth floor alone has 18 fan coil units, and seven floors together have more than 130. Flipping, matching and copying takes half a day, and it is easy to miss something.

    Answering with a knowledge graph takes one query

    If the drawings have already been organised into a network of “who supplies whom, and who is where”, the computer can follow the arrows itself:

    CH-1 → feeds → 18 fan coil units → feed → 13 HVAC zones → contain → 13 rooms

    On the 6F graph this query returns 13 rooms in under a second: 7 faculty offices, 2 graduate student rooms, 2 academic exchange rooms, 1 laboratory and a lounge. The data room is not on the list, because it is supplied by a separate dedicated system.

    In the lab, reachable rooms are called potentially affected. FCU-6F-10 receives chilled water from both CH-1 and CH-2, so whether a room actually warms up also depends on redundancy, operating state and available capacity.

    Run the same walk on the lab’s small demonstration graph and compare it with the full-floor answer.

    §2

    What a knowledge graph is

    A knowledge graph is a network of things and the relationships between them. The things are called nodes; the relationships are named, directed arrows between nodes.

    A map photo versus a navigation road network

    As-built drawings are like a photo of a map: a person can see which road connects to which, but a computer sees only lines and text. A knowledge graph is like the road network data behind a navigation app. Every intersection is a node and every road segment is a relationship, so the computer can answer “how do I get from A to B?”

    For the same HVAC system, drawings store how it is drawn; a knowledge graph stores what it means.

    Triples: one fact in one sentence

    Every fact in the graph is a short sentence of subject, relationship and object, called a triple.

    SubjectRelationshipObjectIn plain words
    CH-1feedsFCU-6F-10Chiller CH-1 supplies fan coil unit 10
    FCU-6F-10locatedInR6-08Fan coil unit 10 is installed in room R6-08
    FCU-6F-10feedsZ-R6-08Fan coil unit 10 supplies HVAC zone Z-R6-08
    FCU-6F-10hasPointFCU-6F-10-RATFan coil unit 10 has a return air temperature point
    FCU-6F-10typeFCUFan coil unit 10 is a fan coil unit

    Connect many such sentences and you get a network. The 6F baseline graph has 210 nodes and about 1,400 triples.

    Why not just use Excel?

    Excel is a set of tables, good for lists. As soon as a question needs several steps across tables, such as chiller to fan coil unit to zone to room, you end up chaining VLOOKUPs. A knowledge graph is made of arrows to begin with, so you simply follow them. The graph also knows that a fan coil unit is a kind of HVAC equipment, so asking for “all HVAC equipment” includes it automatically.

    §3

    Brick and REC: everyone speaking the same language

    If one company writes “small fan unit”, another “FCU” and a third “fan coil”, a computer cannot tell they are the same thing. An ontology is an agreed dictionary: it defines which classes exist, how classes relate as broader and narrower, and which relationships may be used. Civil engineers know the idea from drawing legends and the work item codes of public construction. Once everyone uses the same set, data can pass between companies and software.

    OntologyCoversClasses used in this graph
    Brick Schema 1.4.4Equipment, systems, points, and relationships such as “feeds” and “has point”Chiller, fan coil unit, air handling unit, three-way valve, controller, return air temperature sensor, and more
    RealEstateCore (REC)Building spaces: buildings, levels, room types, HVAC zonesOffice, dry laboratory, data server room, lounge, HVAC zone

    Since Brick 1.4, space classes are handled by REC and the old Brick space classes are deprecated. This graph uses the new forms throughout and has been validated against the official Brick 1.4.4 checking rules.

    Classes are arranged in a hierarchy. A fan coil unit is a kind of HVAC equipment, and HVAC equipment is a kind of equipment. So when you ask the graph to “list all equipment”, the fan coil units are included automatically, without listing them one by one.

    §4

    From drawings to graph: each drawing is one piece of the puzzle

    No single drawing can answer the opening question on its own. Each type of drawing supplies one kind of information, and modelling means joining them together.

    DrawingWhat it knowsWhat it becomes in the graph
    Equipment scheduleWhich equipment exists, models, capacitiesEquipment nodes and attributes such as capacity and airflow
    System flow diagramWhose water goes where; how many units per floor“Feeds” relationships and system groupings
    HVAC floor planWhere each unit is installed“Located in” relationships
    Architectural floor planWhat each space is called and used forRoom nodes and room types (office, laboratory and so on)
    Control schematics and device specificationsHow controllers, sensors and panels are wiredPoint nodes (temperatures, commands, setpoints) and control relationships

    Three stories from the modelling work

    • The blank schedule. The drawings are 2009 AutoCAD files. In an online viewer the equipment schedule showed only table borders with every cell blank, because an old Chinese font was missing. The text was still in the file, and reading it directly with a program solved the problem.
    • The shifted names. Room names are not on the HVAC drawings, which show only area labels. The names are on a separate architectural drawing, and the two drawings were offset by a translation. Only after aligning them did the names of 14 rooms match.
    • Three drawings agree. The plan shows 18 fan coil units, and the flow diagram also lists 18 for 6F. The model distribution inferred from room size (8 larger units, 10 smaller units) matches the flow diagram exactly.
    §5

    Following one fan coil unit: FCU-6F-10

    On the drawing

    On the 6F HVAC plan it is just a small square symbol on the south side, with a “T” symbol for a room panel next to it and a chilled water branch from the corridor main. The drawing gives neither its model nor the name of the room.

    In sentences

    After combining the five types of drawings, we know this about it:

    1. It is a fan coil unit, model FC-8, with a cooling capacity of 6,048 kcal/h and an airflow of 1,360 CMH. ≈model inferred from room size
    2. It is installed in room R6-08, a 25.2 m² faculty office.
    3. It receives chilled water from chillers CH-1 and CH-2 of the central chilled water system.
    4. It supplies HVAC zone Z-R6-08, and that zone contains room R6-08.
    5. It has a motorized three-way valve that controls whether chilled water flows through its coil.
    6. It carries a DFE microcomputer controller that measures return air temperature, switches fan speed, and opens or closes the valve.
    7. On the room wall there is an MSE one-to-one panel where the occupant sets the room temperature. ≈panel type inferred: one unit in the room

    Item 1’s model is inferred from the room size, and item 7’s panel type is inferred from the room having only one fan coil unit. Everything else comes directly from the drawings.

    In the graph file

    The same facts in Turtle, the format the graph file uses (excerpt):

    ntuce:FCU-6F-10  a  brick:FCU ;
    rec:locatedIn          ntuce:R6-08 ;
    brick:feeds            ntuce:Z-R6-08 ;
    brick:hasPart          ntuce:CHWV-6F-10 ;
    brick:hasPoint         ntuce:FCU-6F-10-RAT , ntuce:FCU-6F-10-FSC ;
    bi:modelTag            "FC-8" ;
    bi:designAirflow_CMH   1360 ;
    bi:sourceDocument      "M3,4,5-06F 6F HVAC plan" .

ntuce:CH-1   brick:feeds   ntuce:FCU-6F-10 .
ntuce:R6-08  a  rec:Office ;  bi:roomName  "Faculty office" ;  bi:areaSqm  25.2 .

    It reads naturally. The first line says “FCU-6F-10 is an FCU”, and each following line is “it + relationship + object”. A semicolon means the subject stays the same; a period means the subject is finished.

    How many things this one unit connects to

    One fan coil unit has more than 10 connections: 2 chillers, 1 system, 1 room, 1 zone, 1 valve, 1 controller, 1 panel and 3 points. On paper these are spread across 5 different drawings; in the graph they all attach to one node.

    Click through FCU-6F-10’s neighbourhood and see the source drawing and certainty of every fact.

    §6

    Points: where equipment talks

    Points are the values equipment measures, the commands it receives and the settings people give it. Without points the graph knows only where equipment is. With points, it knows where to read a temperature and where to send a command.

    KindAnalogyExamples in this graph
    SensorEyes: tell you how things are nowFan coil unit return air temperature; cooling tower leaving water temperature
    CommandHands: tell the equipment to actFan speed high, medium, low; valve open or close; chiller start and stop
    SetpointA target: tell the equipment what to reachRoom temperature setting on a panel, such as 26°C
    StatusIndicator lights: running or failed?Whether a pump is running; whether an air handling unit has tripped

    The I/O on control drawings is where points come from

    CodeMeaningCorresponding point
    AIAnalog input: measures a continuous valueSensor, such as temperature
    DIDigital input: reads on or offStatus, such as a trip
    DODigital output: sends on or offCommand, such as start and stop or valve open and close
    AOAnalog output: sends a continuous valueProportional command, such as valve position (not used in this graph)

    For the DFE controller of FCU-6F-10, the control drawing shows AI×1, DO×3 and DO×1, which become three points: AI×1 → return air temperature sensor; DO×3 → fan speed command with high, medium and low; DO×1 → valve command, attached to the three-way valve.

    Across the 18 fan coil units, 13 panels, the data room chilled water system and two packaged air handling units on 6F there are 88 points, every one of them backed by the control drawings.

    Points live in controllers

    Fan coil unit points live in each unit’s DFE. The points of the data room chillers, pumps and cooling towers live in a field controller (DLC) that supports the BACnet communication standard. The graph records this with the hostedBy relationship, so that when real data is connected later, it is clear which controller to read from.

    §7

    Honest labelling: what the drawings say versus what was inferred

    Every node in the graph carries two labels: which drawing it came from, and whether it is written on the drawing or was inferred.

    Assertion statusNodesMeaningExamples
    ■Drawing-derived189Written in black and white on a drawingFan coil unit locations, chiller capacities, faculty office names
    ≈Inferred21Not written directly on a drawing, but reasonably deducedFan coil unit models inferred from room size; the use of three small rooms inferred from area

    Why go to this trouble? The graph will later be used to make decisions. If a diagnostic conclusion happens to rest on an inferred fact, the user should know how reliable that conclusion is. Research calls this provenance. A construction daily log does the same thing when it records numbers measured on site separately from numbers calculated from the design drawings.

    The first version of the graph had 26 points that were “filled in to make the model complete”. After the control drawings were found, all 26 were replaced by points backed by the drawings, and the synthetic part dropped to zero. Clear labels made that possible: you know exactly what can be replaced later.

    The lab adds a third label, illustrative, for everything added only for the demonstration.

    §8

    Validation: a building code review for the graph

    Once the graph is built, how do we know it is not wrong? Write rules and let the computer check automatically. The standard format for such rules is called SHACL. When an architect submits drawings for a permit, the reviewer checks them against the building code item by item. SHACL is a building code for the graph, for example:

    • Every fan coil unit must have a return air temperature point
    • Every fan coil unit must supply some HVAC zone
    • Every fan coil unit must receive chilled water from a chiller
    • The object of “located in” must be a space, not another piece of equipment

    The baseline graph passes every rule shipped with the official Brick 1.4.4. During modelling, the first version produced 55 violations because some relationships were written the wrong way; after correction there were none. The rules are not only a final check; they also teach you while you model.

    Planting errors on purpose

    Researchers deliberately remove or alter facts in the clean graph to imitate errors common in real handovers. This is called defect injection.

    Injected defectReal situation it imitatesWhat the rules should report
    Delete the return air temperature point of FCU-6F-10Handover data omit a sensorFCU-6F-10 lacks a return air temperature point
    Delete the “located in” of a fan coil unitThe equipment list gives no installed locationThat equipment has no location
    Point a fan coil unit’s “feeds” to another room’s zoneThe floor plan and the system drawing disagreeThe zone does not match the room where the equipment is

    Tested result: the clean graph passes the rule “every fan coil unit must have a return air temperature point”. After that point is deleted from FCU-6F-10, the rule immediately reports a violation and names FCU-6F-10 precisely.

    Inject these three defects yourself and watch the SHACL-style checks catch them.

    §9

    Real-world gaps, and the role of this graph in research

    Even official as-built drawings are incomplete

    GapConsequence
    HVAC drawings carry no room names; another drawing had to be aligned by coordinatesLinks between equipment and spaces depend on manual stitching
    Control drawings give I/O counts but no point names, numbers or communication addressesWe know “there is a temperature”, but not where to read it
    A “not in this project” remark in the equipment schedule sits between two rowsThe scope boundary of the works is ambiguous
    The title block of the master index contains text from another projectA drawing template was reused without being cleaned
    The renovation was split into “this phase” and “next phase”Data for the same floor come from different periods

    These are not signs of careless people. Drawings are made to build a building, not to run one. That missing piece is exactly what a knowledge graph is meant to supply.

    SGR: Symbolic-Guided Regeneration

    The lab’s SGR research asks: when a large language model helps repair or update a building’s knowledge graph, how do we make sure it does not make things up?

    1. The language model proposes a change, for example adding a missing point.
    2. SHACL rules check the changed graph.
    3. If there are violations, the model is told where and why it is wrong, and generates the change again.
    4. This repeats until the graph passes, and every step records its provenance.

    To evaluate the method you need an answer key: a real, clean graph with clear provenance. Defects are injected into it first, and then you see whether SGR can repair them. The 6F baseline graph is that answer key.

      About & sources

      What this site is, and what it is not

      6F Knowledge Graph Lab is an educational knowledge-graph simulation built from one source document: From As-Built Drawings to a Knowledge Graph: An Introduction to the 6F Baseline Graph by Chien-Pu Huang (Oct 25, 2026). It is written for third-year undergraduate to first-year master’s students in civil engineering.

      What the site does

      • Shows the documented neighbourhood of FCU-6F-10 as a graph you can pan, zoom and inspect.
      • Lets you inject the three defects from the PDF’s defect table into an in-memory copy of the graph.
      • Runs three SHACL-style demonstration checks on the current graph data.
      • Walks through a simulated SGR repair with deterministic, scripted proposals that you approve or reject.
      • Traces which rooms are reachable from a tripped chiller on the current graph.
      • Exports the experiment as a JSON report.

      What it does not do

      • It does not calculate physical temperature changes or heating times.
      • It does not read from or control any real equipment.
      • It does not recreate the full 210-node graph or the real floor plan; neither the graph file nor the floor-plan geometry was supplied.
      • It does not run a SHACL engine or the official Brick 1.4.4 shapes. The checks are JavaScript written to behave like SHACL shapes.
      • It does not run a language model and needs no API key. The SGR proposals are scripted.

      Three kinds of information

      ■Drawing-derived

      Facts the PDF reports as written on the as-built drawings, such as FCU-6F-10 being installed in R6-08, or R6-08 being a 25.2 m² faculty office.

      ≈Inferred

      Deductions the PDF names. The FC-8 model is inferred from the room size, so its capacity and airflow apply only through that inference. The MSE one-to-one panel type is inferred from the room having a single fan coil unit.

      ◇Illustrative

      Added for the demonstration: FCU-6F-05 and FCU-6F-09 with their rooms and zones, the data-room chain, and identifiers the PDF does not give.

      Identifiers

      These identifiers appear in the PDF exactly as written:

      CH-1CH-2FCU-6F-10FCU-6F-10-RATFCU-6F-10-FSCCHWV-6F-10R6-08Z-R6-08

      Every other identifier in the lab is illustrative and drawn with a dashed outline, for example DFE-6F-10, PNL-R6-08, CHWV-6F-10-VC, CHWS-C, L6F and DR-CH-1. Some stand for things the PDF does report (the DFE controller, the MSE panel, the valve command, the central chilled water system) but without a name.

      How the lab works

      • An immutable baseline graph is built once and frozen. Every experiment works on a separate mutable copy.
      • Defects really change that copy. Removed facts are shown as red ghost edges and are excluded from the graph data.
      • Validation and the chiller traversal are computed from the current graph data each time, not from scenario flags.
      • Repair proposals are kept apart from applied changes. A patch only touches the graph after you approve it, and a patch that fails revalidation is rolled back.
      • Repairs cite baseline evidence. If evidence is withheld, or the attempt limit is reached, the violation is escalated for human review instead of being guessed.
      • Every step is written to the event timeline and can be exported as JSON.

      Acceptance checks

      These run the lab’s own engine on a separate scratch experiment, so your current experiment is not touched.
